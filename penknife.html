01 / Context

A staffing operation running on spreadsheets and friction

314e Corporation is a US-based healthcare IT company in the SF Bay Area. Their core business includes running large staffing operations for hospital systems — managing hundreds of open clinical positions simultaneously. When I joined the project, they were doing it with fragments: a generic ATS, a disconnected CRM, email threads, and manual spreadsheet pipelines.

Penknife was 314e's answer — their own proprietary platform. Its real commercial model was white-labelling: hospitals get a fully branded recruitment portal (their logo, their domain, their colours), while 314e operates the whole system behind the scenes. UCLA Health is one known deployment.

That white-label model had a direct design consequence: we couldn't lean on a strong product identity as a quality signal. The experience itself had to carry the weight. The tool had to be recognisably good, not recognisably Penknife.

02 / The Problem

Healthcare recruiting isn't like other recruiting

The complexity is significantly higher. Candidates carry multi-layered credential requirements — licence types, specialties, compliance documentation. Positions are often urgent; a clinical coverage gap directly affects patient care. And the regulatory environment adds verification layers that generic platforms were never built to handle.

  • Four disconnected tools

    ATS for tracking. A separate CRM for client relationships. Email for communication. Spreadsheets for reporting. No single source of truth.

  • No unified candidate view

    Recruiter context was scattered across systems. Every handoff meant re-explaining a candidate's history from scratch.

  • Healthcare complexity unaddressed

    Generic platforms had no concept of licence types, specialty matching, or compliance documentation workflows.

  • Hospital clients had no visibility

    Hiring managers at client hospitals had no real-time view into their open positions. Status updates were compiled manually each week.

"How do you design a single platform that makes a recruiter's full day — from sourcing to placement — faster, less error-prone, and contextually aware of healthcare's unique demands?"

03 / Stakeholders & Users

Three very different relationships with the same data

Penknife serves a layered stakeholder map. Getting the IA right meant understanding that these users don't just have different needs — they have different tolerances for complexity and completely different session patterns.

Primary · Power User

Healthcare Recruiters

314e's internal team. They live in the platform all day — sourcing, pipeline management, interview scheduling, client submissions, offer handling. They need speed, minimal context-switching, and a system that picks up where they left off.

Primary · Occasional User

Hiring Managers

Hospital-side. They log in to review submitted candidates, leave feedback, and approve hires. They need clarity, not complexity. Their view is a stripped-down subset of the full recruiter interface.

Indirect · Experience User

Candidates

Applicants who interact with the white-labelled portal. They never see Penknife — they see the hospital's branded careers page. But their data flows directly into Penknife's ATS. The portal quality is a direct output of how well Penknife's job posting flow was designed.

Secondary: Account Managers at 314e (pipeline visibility), Compliance teams (credentialing trails), IT/Engineering (existing codebase constraints).

04 / Design Process

Research, then architecture, then screens

We started with direct access to 314e's own recruitment team — an advantage because we had real users from day one, and a constraint because internal projects carry political weight. Not every pain point was freely shared in group settings. We had to do contextual inquiry: sitting with recruiters during their actual workday, not just interviewing them.

Contextual inquiry with senior recruiters Workflow mapping (applied → placed) Competitive audit — iCIMS, Workday, Lever, Bullhorn Stakeholder interviews with account managers Spreadsheet mental model analysis

Key insight: the ATS/CRM split was the core problem

The most important finding: ATS and CRM functions were not separable in a recruiter's mind. A candidate is also a relationship. A job opening is also a client deliverable. Every recruiter described constantly toggling between tracking where a candidate was in the process (ATS thinking) and managing what the client knew or expected (CRM thinking).

Generic platforms forced this split. Penknife's design premise was to collapse it entirely — treat the recruiter's day as a unified workflow, not two systems bolted together.

04a / Information Architecture

Five objects. Every feature is a relationship between them.

Given the scale of data Penknife handles — candidates, jobs, clients, interviews, tasks, communications, compliance docs — IA was the most critical phase. We ran card-sorting and tree-testing sessions with recruiters to understand how they naturally grouped information, rather than how we assumed they did.

Candidates Jobs Clients Tasks Reports
Role-Aware Dashboard Assigned jobs and today's schedule at session start

The dashboard surfaces each recruiter's own assigned jobs with live application counts (220, 29, 56…). The right column anchors the calendar and today's scheduled interviews — the two highest time-sensitive priorities. No navigation needed from a cold open.

Object-Based Sidebar Data entities in the nav, not feature names

The sidebar lists Candidates, Jobs, Applications, Placements, Tasks, Contacts, Companies, Opportunities — every screen is a filtered view of one of these objects. Card-sorting confirmed recruiters navigate by what they're looking for, not by which workflow step they're on.

04b / Wireframing & Iteration

Kanban first, table view second — because that's how recruiters think

We moved quickly through lo-fi wireframes because recruiters responded far better to something clickable than to static screens. Early prototypes were tested in-session, iterated same-day.

A key early decision: we prototyped the Kanban view before the table view. It mapped better to the mental model recruiters described — "I think of candidates as moving through stages." The table view came later as a power-user alternative, once we understood that high-volume recruiters preferred scanning rows.

Mental Model Mapping Kanban first — driven by how recruiters speak

During contextual inquiry, recruiters consistently used stage language — "she's at interview," "he's been submitted," "still in screening." We built the Kanban view first, before the table was even scoped. The visual model matched how they already thought about candidates.

Stage as the Action Moving a card is the status update

Column placement is the status action — Screened → Qualified → Interviewed → Final Selection. No separate dropdown, no edit step. Reduces status updates from a three-click flow to a single drag. Recruiters in testing commented on this without being prompted.

Candidate pipeline — Kanban stage view

Table as Power-User Mode High-volume pipelines need row scanning, not cards

Recruiters managing 30–40 candidates per role needed to compare across dimensions quickly — applied date, stage, fit score, scheduled time — without drilling into individual records. The table view (Applications tab) is the alt view for this. Both modes ship; the toggle lives on the job record.

Context-Staying Tab Structure All views of a job stay under one record

Applications, Details, Activities, Draft Applications, and AI Recommendations are tabs within the same job record — no context loss when switching views. A recruiter stays anchored to the job while accessing any mode of working with it.

Job applications — table view

04c / UI Design & System Building

Calm, professional, token-based — built to be invisible

We used a muted blue and neutral palette deliberately. Healthcare environments have enough visual stress. The tool should feel like a steady, controlled workspace — not an exciting startup product. Typography was optimised for dense data readability: clean sans-serif, strict hierarchy for data labels, field values, status badges, and action affordances.

Given the white-label requirement, we implemented a proper design token system. Colour tokens were abstract (primary, secondary, surface, etc.) so swapping a client's brand in was a token swap, not a redesign. For UCLA's deployment, onboarding time was under two days.

CRM Surface, Same Platform A different home for a different role

Account managers and relationship owners land here — not the recruiter dashboard. "Followed Records" surfaces watched contacts; the three widget panels show today's meetings and recently interacted contacts. Same data model, different role lens. Zero re-platforming needed to switch contexts.

Context Restoration Where you left off, not where you started

The home surface is designed around relationship recency — showing the contacts and meetings that are currently active. Every session starts with what matters now. The same principle as the recruiter dashboard, applied to the CRM workflow.

05 / Key Features

Six features that define the unified workflow

01

Unified Candidate + Client Timeline

The core experience innovation. Every candidate record shows not just application history, but every touchpoint with the associated client — emails sent, interviews scheduled, feedback received. A recruiter opening a candidate file sees the full story, not half of it. This was the most direct design expression of the ATS/CRM unification insight.

Communication History In-Record Email opens and outbound threads logged automatically

The Activities tab on a contact record shows every email opened, every outbound message sent — timestamped and attributed to the sender. No manual logging, no separate email tool. The recruiter opens the record and the full communication history is already there.

CRM + ATS in One Panel Relationship context lives alongside contact data

The detail panel surfaces company affiliation, deal value, and contact metadata (left) alongside the live activity feed (right). A recruiter — or account manager — gets the full picture without switching between a CRM and a separate activity log.

Contact record — unified activity timeline

02

Kanban Pipeline View

Candidates move through stages: Applied → Screened → Submitted → Interview → Offer → Placed. The Kanban board makes this visible at a glance across an entire job's pipeline. Cards are dense with information — name, specialty, submission date, client feedback status — without becoming cluttered.

We tested multiple card densities. Recruiters handling 20–40 candidates per job needed the compact version. Those doing senior or specialised roles preferred expanded cards with more context visible without clicking in. Both modes shipped.

Stage Columns, Not Lists Pipeline state visible at a glance

Opportunities move through Qualifying → Evaluating → Socializing → Negotiating. The column view makes the full pipeline health scannable — how much is in each stage, which accounts are advancing, where things are stuck — without opening individual records.

Card-Level Data Value and labels visible on the card itself

Each card shows the hospital client name, revenue value, location tags, and action shortcuts inline. Account managers can act directly from the board — no click-through required for the most common actions.

Opportunities pipeline — Kanban view

Detail Without Leaving the Board Slide-out panel keeps Kanban context visible

Clicking an opportunity opens a detail panel over the board — not a new page. The Kanban stays visible in the background. The account manager reads deal details, contact info, and activity log without losing their place in the pipeline view.

Automated Activity Log Discovery events captured without manual entry

The Activities tab records system-generated events (discovery completions, stage changes, email sends) as they happen. The account manager's last touchpoint and current opportunity state are always reconstructable from the record — no manual logging required.

Opportunity detail — slide-out panel with activity log

03

AI-Powered Candidate Scoring

Penknife includes an AI scoring layer that evaluates candidates against job requirements and surfaces a fit score. For healthcare roles, this means the model accounts for licence type, specialty match, years of experience in specific clinical settings, and location/availability constraints.

The design challenge was transparency. Recruiters did not want a black-box score — they wanted to understand why a candidate was ranked where they were. We designed a score breakdown panel showing which factors contributed and at what weight, so recruiters could apply judgement on top of the algorithmic recommendation rather than deferring to it.

Score as a Filter, Not a Ranking Recruiter sets the threshold — algorithm doesn't decide

The "Filter by AI Scoring" panel lets the recruiter set a minimum fit score cutoff (shown: 65 out of 100). Only candidates above that threshold surface. The recruiter controls the cut-off — the AI narrows the pool, but the recruiter defines what "narrow" means.

AI Recommendations as Opt-In A separate tab, not the default view

AI Recommendations is one of five tabs on the job record — the default opens to all Applications unfiltered. Recruiters choose when to apply the AI lens. This was a deliberate positioning decision: the algorithm is a tool the recruiter reaches for, not a filter imposed on entry.

AI Recommendations — fit score filter panel

04

Hospital-Specific External Job Board Integration

One of the standout features from a service design perspective. When a recruiter creates a job in Penknife, it can be published directly to the corresponding hospital's white-labelled careers page — the same flow as traditional job boards, but managed entirely from within Penknife. Candidates applying through the hospital's site have their application routed automatically into the ATS, with the hospital's branding intact throughout.

This was the most technically complex feature and required close collaboration with engineering to define the data model for multi-tenant job publishing. It eliminated the need for recruiters to manually pull applications from external boards and re-enter them into the system.

Hospital Brand, Penknife Invisible Candidate experience is fully white-labelled

Candidates see Fairview Hospital's logo, colour scheme, and domain — no Penknife branding anywhere in the candidate-facing flow. The careers page, job listings, and application forms are all hospital-branded while the entire backend is Penknife's ATS. Powered by Penknife appears only in the footer.

Filtered Job Board by Default Location, specialty, and type filters on the listing page

The careers page ships with left-rail filters for location, specialty, hiring type, and deployment type. These are driven by the structured job fields set in Penknife — not free-text. Healthcare-specific filtering (specialty, deployment) works because the underlying data model was built for it.

Fairview Hospital — white-label careers portal

Job Detail as Candidate Experience Full description, metadata, and similar jobs in one page

The white-label job detail page surfaces the full job description alongside structured metadata (employment type, department, work type, hiring type) in a right sidebar. Similar jobs at the bottom extend the candidate session — a recruiter-designed candidate UX, not just a form.

Inbound Application Routing Applications flow into the ATS automatically

When a candidate applies through this page, a new record is created in Penknife's ATS tagged to the correct job and hospital client — no CSV import, no manual entry. This was the highest-ROI workflow improvement we identified across the entire platform.

White-label job detail — candidate-facing view

05

Mobile — Critical Actions Only

The product is fundamentally a desktop tool. Recruiters work at desks managing large data sets across wide screens. Mobile was never intended to be the primary experience, and we made that call deliberately early — resisting pressure to "make it work on mobile too."

What we designed instead was a constrained mobile view for account managers and hospital-side hiring managers who occasionally need to check status or approve a submission away from their desks: view submitted candidates, leave feedback, approve or reject. Nothing more. The mobile experience is less capable by design, and that was the right call.

Research-Backed Scope

94% desktop sessions — data backed the call

Analytics showed 94% of recruiter sessions on desktop with an average session length of 40+ minutes. A full mobile port would have compromised the desktop experience without delivering a genuinely useful mobile one. We chose to do one exceptionally well.

Hiring Manager Use Case

Three tasks, constrained by design

The mobile surface covers: view submitted candidates, leave feedback, approve or reject. That's the entire feature set. Not a compressed version of the recruiter UI — a purpose-built surface for the hiring manager's away-from-desk scenario. Intentionally less capable than desktop.

06

Reporting & Pipeline Analytics

Account managers needed to show hospital clients pipeline health without giving them full access to the platform. We designed a reporting module that generated client-facing summaries — time-to-fill, submission-to-offer ratios, pipeline velocity by role — formatted for sharing in client meetings.

Internally, 314e used the same data to track recruiter performance and identify pipeline bottlenecks. This was the module I'm most critical of in retrospect — under-designed relative to its operational importance, launched under pressure, and required significant post-launch iteration.

Dashboard as Proxy for Reporting Pipeline counts visible without a reporting module

The dashboard surfaces per-job application volumes (220, 29, 56, 10), open job count (8), and scheduled interview load. This was what hospital hiring managers actually wanted visibility into — their pipeline health at a glance, without going through an account manager for a weekly update.

Known Post-Launch Debt Aggregate counts, not analytics

The dashboard gave surface-level counts but not the deeper metrics clients needed — time-to-fill, submission-to-offer ratios, pipeline velocity by role. The gap between what was built and what was asked for became apparent post-launch. The reporting module needed more design investment than it received under timeline pressure.

06 / Design Decisions & Trade-offs

Four decisions that shaped the product

Platform Strategy

Desktop-first was non-negotiable

Early conversations surfaced pressure to "make it work on mobile too." We pushed back with data: 94% of recruiter sessions were on desktop, average session length was 40+ minutes. A responsive reflow would have been a worse desktop experience and still a mediocre mobile one. We chose to do desktop exceptionally well.

Theming Architecture

Design tokens over CSS overrides

We could have handled white-labelling at the CSS level — just swap a hex code. We chose to implement a proper token system, which added time upfront but made each new client deployment significantly faster. UCLA's onboarding time was under two days. The investment compounded across every subsequent deployment.

Architectural Compromise

Integrated client profile vs. dedicated parent

One of the trickier calls: whether to build a dedicated parent/client profile separate from candidates, or integrate client context into the existing candidate view framework. Building separate would have been more thorough but required significant backend changes and delayed launch. We integrated — which meant UX compromises in client-facing views. A known unresolved issue.

Research Constraint

Research skewed toward 314e's internal team

Most of our research access was with 314e's own recruiters. Hospital-side hiring managers were harder to access during the research phase. This created a blind spot in the client-facing views that became apparent only after deployment. More direct access earlier would have changed several design decisions in the approval and feedback flows.

07 / What I'd Do Differently

The honest parts

This is a live product I'm genuinely proud of. It also has real seams. These are the things I'd change.

  • Better sprint structure during discovery. We front-loaded research into a long, unstructured phase — no sprint cadence, no time-boxed synthesis loops. Good insight, poor momentum going into the design phase, which created pressure at the back end. I'd run discovery in shorter, tighter cycles now.

  • More direct access to hospital hiring managers, earlier. The hiring manager view — client-facing portal and approval flows — was designed largely from inference and secondary interviews. We could see the blind spot forming and didn't have the access to close it in time.

  • The reporting module needed more time. It became a major client request that we had to iterate quickly, under pressure, after launch. With more design attention during the main build, we could have shipped a significantly more useful reporting layer — the most under-resourced feature relative to its actual strategic importance.

  • The parent profile decision was the wrong call. Integrating client context into the candidate view framework was pragmatic but produced UX compromises we knew about at the time. Given the same timeline constraints, I'd push harder to carve out the backend work — the integrated approach has a ceiling that limits how good the client-facing experience can get.

08 / Outcomes

Directional results from a live, deployed product

Penknife is in production across multiple US hospital networks. Specific metrics are owned by 314e and not all are public — what follows is directional, not a press release.

  • Significant reduction in time-to-fill Consolidating four systems into one removed the re-entry overhead and context-reconstruction lag built into every recruiter's daily workflow.
  • Recruiter onboarding time decreased The platform was intuitive enough for new 314e hires to become productive faster — a reduction in formal training overhead observed consistently across new recruiter cohorts.
  • Hospital client satisfaction improved Primarily attributed to real-time pipeline visibility. Hiring managers could check their open positions without chasing account managers for weekly status updates.
  • White-label deployment time cut significantly The token-based theming system took each new client deployment from a multi-week integration project to under two days. UCLA Health's deployment was completed in under 48 hours.
  • AI scoring became a sales differentiator The explainability-first approach — score breakdowns with contributing factors — became a feature 314e's account managers actively led with in pitches to new hospital network partners.
